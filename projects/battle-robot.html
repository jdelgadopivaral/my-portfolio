Mechanical Design & Manufacturing · Completed

Battle Robot

Designed and manufactured a competition robot through CAD, analysis, machining, fabrication, assembly, and iterative testing for Harvard Engineering Sciences 51.

Goal

The goal was to design and build a remote-controlled robot for the ES51 Turf Wars competition. The robot had to fit inside an 11 in × 11 in × 11 in starting box, climb 15° and 30° ramps, collect irregular objects, and score them into elevated goals during four-minute matches.

My Contribution

I contributed throughout the robot’s design and build process, including concept development, CAD, manufacturing, assembly, and system integration. I helped develop the drivetrain, gearbox, wheels, arm, and structural components and assembled most of the final robot as the individual systems came together.

Result

The finished robot successfully climbed the 30° ramp, reliably scored in the 7-inch and 14-inch tiers, and finished 1st out of 6 teams in the Harvard Engineering Sciences 51 final competition.

Overview

LeBot James was a compact four-wheel robot built around a two-bar arm and claw mechanism. The final design balanced simplicity, reliability, manufacturability, and scoring performance while staying within the competition’s size and manufacturing constraints.

SolidWorks · Machining · Laser Cutting · 3D Printing · Casting · Assembly

Design Process

We explored several drivetrain and arm concepts before simplifying the design into a four-wheel robot with a two-bar arm. Early sketches, physical prototypes, and CAD helped us refine the overall geometry before committing to the final assembly.

Final CAD

After the overall concept was finalized, we developed the robot as a complete SolidWorks assembly and refined the individual subsystems around the competition constraints. The final CAD helped us verify packaging, arm reach, drivetrain layout, gearbox placement, and how the individual components would fit together before manufacturing.

Engineering Analysis

Before manufacturing the final parts, we used engineering analysis to check whether the design could handle the expected competition loads. We evaluated drivetrain performance, arm loading, reach, stability, and structural strength.

One of the checks included a SolidWorks Simulation of the acrylic arm support under the expected lifting load. The analysis showed that the stress remained well below the material’s yield strength and that the arm experienced only a small amount of deflection.

Manufacturing

The final robot combined several manufacturing processes, including laser-cut acrylic structures, 3D-printed claw components, machined shafts and supports, and Ecoflex cast wheels.

Drivetrain Testing

Cardboard Drivetrain Mockup

Before manufacturing the final chassis, we built a cardboard drivetrain mockup with the drivetrain and gearbox components mounted in place. This gave us a quick way to evaluate the basic layout, motor and gearbox placement, wheel spacing, and overall drivetrain behavior before committing to the acrylic structure.

Final Acrylic Drivetrain

After the final drivetrain components were manufactured, we assembled the acrylic drivetrain as a complete subsystem and tested the actual geometry, wheel alignment, gearbox mounting, and drivetrain operation before integrating the rest of the robot.

Assembly & Integration

Once the drivetrain had been tested and the remaining components were manufactured, we began integrating the full robot. I assembled most of the final system and worked through fit-up, alignment, hardware, and integration issues between the drivetrain, gearbox, arm, claw, and structure.

Competition / Result

The final robot successfully climbed the 15° and 30° ramps, handled multiple competition objects, and reliably scored in the 7-inch and 14-inch tiers.

Our team finished 1st out of 6 teams in the Harvard Engineering Sciences 51 final competition.

What I Learned

This project was one of my first experiences taking a mechanical system all the way from early sketches to a finished machine. The biggest takeaway for me was how much the design changes once manufacturing and assembly become real constraints. Parts that looked straightforward in CAD still needed to be machined, aligned, assembled, and tested as part of a larger system.

It also gave me much more experience with SolidWorks, machining, fabrication, mechanical assembly, and working through problems as they came up during a build.
